# New Yorker Caption Best Practices

Research-backed principles from Bob Mankoff (former New Yorker cartoon editor), Lawrence Wood (8-time contest winner), and statistical analysis of shortlisted vs. rejected captions.

## The Numbers

- Shortlisted captions average **8.7 words**. Finalists ~10 words. Over 12 = likely padded.
- Captions with **no commas, no question marks, no exclamation points** are significantly more likely to shortlist. Punctuation props up weak jokes — a great caption needs none.

## Core Rules

**Don't describe the image.** The drawing does the visual work. If there's a desert in the drawing, don't use the words "sand" or "cactus" — that's wasted real estate.

**Address the incongruity directly.** Every New Yorker cartoon has a weird or absurd element. The caption's job is to resolve or reframe that incongruity — not ignore it.

**Abstract over concrete.** Winning captions refer to psychological states, relationships, and social dynamics rather than the literal objects in the drawing. Harder to visualize physically = more likely to shortlist.

**Accept the premise; apply mundane logic.** Don't fight the bizarre setup. Accept the characters are aliens, aristocrats, or cavemen — then ask: what's the most human, everyday thing they'd say in this moment?

**Novelty beats familiarity.** If your first-instinct caption uses common, obvious words for the setting, discard it. Hundreds of entrants already submitted it. Uncommon words and unexpected angles shortlist more often.

## Structural Formats That Work

| Format | Description | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| Normal line / crazy situation | Apply mundane everyday logic to an absurd premise | "My doctor told me I have to give up walking." (said by a caveman) |
| Remedy the situation | Desperately apply practical logic to an impossible reality | "Why don't you just admit it — this is the wrong desert." |
| Elegant wordplay | Double meaning linked directly to the visual incongruity | "Do you mind if I bounce something off you?" (speaker to a man with a brick wall for a head) |

## Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Why It Fails |
|---------|-------------|
| Over-explaining | Tells the reader what to think instead of letting them bridge the gap |
| Too clever / too obscure | Inside joke the general reader can't grasp instantly |
| Describing the image | Redundant; the drawing already shows it |
| Wrong speaker | Caption assigned to the wrong character; should go to the visible mouth |
| Over-punctuated | Exclamation points and question marks signal the caption can't stand on its own |
| Over-abstract | Pushed so far into nonsense it fails to ground the visual incongruity |

## Writing Process

1. **Write 10–20 captions** to exhaust the obvious takes
2. **Discard the first 3–5** — they're almost always the clichéd angle
3. **Cut every word that doesn't do work** — if you can remove it without losing the joke, remove it
4. **Strip punctuation last** — reframe to declarative if needed
5. **Read it aloud** — if the delivery has to be theatrical to land, rewrite it

## Applying to This Project

The Verbalized Sampling route pass in Step 1 of the skill is designed to exhaust obvious routes before writing candidates. Use these principles as a filter in Step 2:

- Target ≤10 words per candidate
- At least one candidate must be fully declarative (no comma, no question mark)
- Flag any candidate that describes the image literally
- The "lowest-probability route" candidate should test the abstract/psychological angle
